# Filing types and how to read them

Filing names differ by jurisdiction; the underlying document types are broadly
universal. Read the filing history as a timeline first, then the individual
documents.

## Formation documents

**What they are:** articles or memorandum of association, certificate of
incorporation, operating agreement or bylaws where filed.

**What to extract:** incorporation date and jurisdiction, initial officers and
subscribers, initial share capital and share classes, the stated objects or
business purpose, and the filing agent.

**What it tells you that the summary page doesn't:** the *initial* subscribers.
Whoever incorporated the company is frequently no longer visible in the current
record, and formation agents subscribe on a client's behalf and transfer
immediately. A first-day-to-second-day share transfer is the agent handing over.

**Reading the objects clause:** in modern registries it is usually generic
boilerplate and means nothing. Where a specific business purpose is stated and
it does not match what the company claims to do, that is worth a question.

## Annual return / confirmation statement

**What it is:** a periodic declaration that the registry's held data is correct.

**What to extract:** officers as at the date, registered office, share capital
and shareholders where the jurisdiction requires them, and the declared business
activity code.

**How to read it:** compare consecutive years. The change between two returns is
the information; the return itself is a snapshot. A shareholder list that changes
without a corresponding transfer filing is a discrepancy worth chasing.

**Failure mode:** these are confirmations, not audits. They are filed by whoever
administers the company, frequently in bulk by an agent, and errors persist for
years because nobody reads them.

## Annual accounts / financial statements

**Calibrate to the filing regime before you read anything into them.** A large or
listed company files audited statements with notes, segment reporting, and
related-party disclosure. A small or micro-entity company in most jurisdictions
files an abridged balance sheet, unaudited, with no profit-and-loss account and
no turnover figure. **Absence of revenue disclosure in a small private company is
the legal norm, not evasion.** Saying otherwise in a report is the fastest way to
lose credibility with anyone who knows the regime.

**What to extract even from thin accounts:**

- Whether the company filed as dormant. A dormant filing is a strong, unambiguous
  statement that there was no significant accounting transaction — directly
  contradicting any claim of trading in that period.
- Net assets and whether they are negative.
- Director loans and amounts owed to or from related parties, which are often
  disclosed even in abridged accounts.
- Employee numbers, disclosed in many small-company regimes and a good reality
  check on headcount claims.
- The accountant or auditor named on the filing.

**Signals in the auditor line:** a qualified opinion, an emphasis-of-matter or
going-concern paragraph, an auditor resignation, or repeated auditor changes.
These are rare enough to be meaningful, and they are the closest thing to an
insider warning that a public filing contains.

**Filing punctuality:** read the filed date against the due date across several
years. Consistently late is a governance signal. A sudden shift from timely to
late frequently precedes trouble.

## Officer appointments and resignations

**What to extract:** name, role, date, and — where published — nationality,
country of residence, occupation, service address, and birth month and year.

**How to read it:**

- A mass resignation on one date, especially close to an accounts filing or an
  auditor change, is the highest-signal event in a filing history.
- A director appointed and resigned within weeks was usually never operationally
  involved.
- A director whose service address is the accountant's, whose occupation is blank
  or "director", and who appears across dozens of unrelated companies is a
  professional or nominee director.
- A secretary role held by a corporate services firm is ordinary and carries no
  signal.
- Compare the registry officers against the website's leadership page. People on
  the site but not the register have no legal responsibility; people on the
  register but not the site are the ones nobody wanted you to ask about.

## Share allotments, transfers, and capital changes

**What to extract:** who received or gave up shares, when, in what class, and at
what stated consideration.

**How to read it:** share classes matter more than share counts. A small holding
of a class carrying enhanced voting or veto rights controls a company that a
naive shareholder-percentage reading says it does not. Where the register
publishes articles or class rights, read them — the beneficial ownership
declaration may be technically accurate and substantively misleading because it
counts capital rather than control.

Consideration stated as nominal on a transfer of a valuable business usually
means the transfer is intra-group or to a related party, not that the business is
worthless.

## Charges, mortgages, liens, and security interests

Chronically overlooked and often the most informative filing available.

**What to extract:** the secured party, the date created and the date registered,
what assets are secured, and whether the charge has been satisfied or released.

**How to read it:**

- The secured lender is a counterparty who did their own diligence and took
  security. Who they are tells you about the company's financing.
- A charge created shortly before an insolvency event is a specific and
  well-known pattern.
- All-assets floating charges give the lender substantial practical control.
- A satisfied charge means the debt was repaid; an unsatisfied one may just mean
  nobody filed the release.
- A company claiming substantial operations with no financing history and no
  filed accounts showing activity has an unexplained source of funds.

## Beneficial ownership declarations

**What to extract:** the declared natural persons, their nature of control (share
ownership, voting rights, right to appoint directors, or other significant
influence), the date of the declaration, and any statement that no owner was
identified.

**How to read it:** the *nature of control* field carries more information than
the identity. Control asserted through the right to appoint directors, rather
than through shareholding, indicates a governance arrangement sitting outside the
share register. A declaration naming a corporate entity rather than a person
means the chain continues; follow it. A declaration of "no person identified" is
sometimes legitimate and usually means nobody walked the chain.

Cross-check the declaration against the share register. They should reconcile.
When they don't, one of them is wrong, and that is a finding.

## Registered office and name changes

Low signal individually, useful in combination. A change of registered office
coinciding with a change of officers and a share transfer is a change of control
whether or not anyone filed it as one. A name change shortly after adverse
publicity is a specific pattern worth checking against media archives, and it is
why you always collect former names before running litigation and adverse-media
searches.

## Insolvency and strike-off filings

**What to extract:** the procedure type, the appointed office-holder, the dates,
and — where published — the office-holder's reports.

**How to read it:** the procedure type is the whole story. Voluntary dissolution
of a solvent company is unremarkable. Compulsory strike-off, compulsory
liquidation, and administration are not. Office-holders' reports, where public,
often contain a narrative of what went wrong, a creditor list, and comment on
directors' conduct — the most candid document that will ever exist about that
company, and hardly anyone reads it.

## Listed-company filings

A different universe. Annual and interim reports, material-event disclosures,
insider and substantial-holding notifications, prospectuses, and proxy or general
meeting materials.

The two exhibits investigators under-use: the **subsidiary list** attached to the
annual report, which hands you the group structure that would otherwise take days
of registry work, and the **related-party transactions note**, which names the
entities and people the company transacts with off the open market.

Full-text search across a securities regulator's filing corpus lets you find your
target named inside *someone else's* filings — as a counterparty, a customer, a
litigant, or a director. That is frequently how a private company becomes visible.

## Reading the timeline

Before any individual document, list every filing with its date and type and look
for:

| Pattern | Likely meaning |
|---|---|
| Incorporation shortly before a tender, contract, or pitch | The entity has no track record; the claimed history belongs elsewhere |
| Officer resignations clustered on one date | Governance rupture; check what was filed either side |
| Auditor change followed by late accounts | Disagreement over the accounts |
| Share transfer plus office change plus new directors | Change of control |
| Dormant filings followed by sudden activity | A shelf company being brought into use |
| A batch of filings all submitted on one day | An agent remediating a compliance failure, often before a transaction |
| Long gap then a strike-off notice then a restoration | Usually litigation — a creditor restored the company to sue it |
